# Coors founding story: verified facts

Compiled 2026-10-10 for the ~45 s collage film. Each item gives the claim, the sources, and a confidence rating.
Confidence: **High** means two or more independent reliable sources agree. **Medium** means one good source, or sources that differ on details. **Low** means weakly sourced or contradicted.
Flags: ⚠ disputed or commonly misstated. 🔒 sensitive.

## 1. Adolph Coors: early life in Germany

| # | Claim | Sources | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Born **February 4, 1847**, in **Barmen, Rhenish Prussia** (today part of Wuppertal). Birth name **Adolph Herman Joseph Kuhrs**; some sources spell it "Kohrs". He later Americanized it to "Coors". | WP, MC-Stowaway, HC-Kids ("Kohrs") | High (date and place). ⚠ Spelling of the original surname varies (Kuhrs / Kohrs). |
| 1.2 | The family moved to **Dortmund**. WP says this was around 1862. MC-Stowaway says he was about one year old, with his father Joseph described as a master miller. | WP, MC-Stowaway | Medium. ⚠ Sources disagree on the timing. |
| 1.3 | Apprenticed in **July 1862** at Heinrich (Henry) **Wenker's** brewery in Dortmund. WP calls it "Kronen". MC-Stowaway places it across the street from the family home. The apprenticeship ran three years, and he paid his fee by working as the brewery's bookkeeper. He stayed on as a paid worker until **May 1867**, then worked at breweries in Kassel, Berlin and Uelzen. | WP, MC-Stowaway, HC-Kids ("apprentice at a brewery when he was fifteen") | High. ⚠ His age at apprenticeship is given as 14 (MC-Stowaway) or 15 (HC-Kids, CBHF). |
| 1.4 | Both parents died in 1862: his mother Helena on April 2, and his father Joseph on November 24. MC-Stowaway says he and two younger siblings went to an orphanage. | WP; MC-Stowaway ("a year later, both parents died of illness") | High that he was orphaned as a teenager. Medium on the exact dates. |
| 1.5 | Before brewing, he was a stationery and book-store apprentice in Ruhrort (1860–62). HC-Kids instead says "printer's assistant". | WP, HC-Kids | Medium |

## 2. Emigration (1868)

| # | Claim | Sources | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | He emigrated in **1868**, aged 21, as a **stowaway**. MC-Stowaway adds that he was facing possible conscription into the Prussian army. | WP, MC-Stowaway, HC-Kids, CBHF, FaG | High |
| 2.2 | ⚠ **Port of arrival is disputed.** Molson Coors's own blog, History Colorado, CBHF and Find a Grave say **Baltimore, Maryland**. Wikipedia says he sailed **Hamburg → New York City**. No source names the ship. Recommendation: use Baltimore, which the company itself uses, or keep the caption generic ("stowed away to America, 1868"). | MC-Stowaway, HC-Kids, CBHF, FaG vs WP | Medium (Baltimore) |
| 2.3 | He arrived penniless and spoke no English. He spent his first year paying off the debt for his passage, and he was ashamed of stowing away all his life. The family reportedly did not discuss it openly until his son's death in 1970. | HC-Kids, MC-Stowaway, WP | Medium-High |
| 2.4 | Jobs before Naperville: **bricklayer (apprentice), stonecutter, fireman** (shovelling coal into a steam-engine firebox, *not* a firefighter), hoisting engineer, and pick-and-shovel laborer. MC-Stowaway places the laborer job on the Chicago drainage canal. WP has him reaching **Chicago on May 30, 1868**, working as a laborer and summer brewer in 1868, a fireman in winter 1868–69, and a bricklayer and stonecutter in spring–summer 1869. | WP, MC-Stowaway | High. ⚠ "Fireman" means a **stoker** on a steam engine. Avoid fire-brigade imagery for the caption, or use it knowingly. |

## 3. Naperville and Denver (1869–1872)

| # | Claim | Sources | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Became foreman, later "General Brewery Superintendent", of **John Stenger's brewery, Naperville, Illinois** (about 30–35 miles west of Chicago) on **August 11, 1869**, aged 22. | WP, MC-Stowaway | High |
| 3.2 | Resigned **January 22, 1872**. Family lore offers three reasons: Stenger wanted him to marry one of his three daughters; a jilted romance; or Coors lacked the capital to buy into the brewery. | WP, MC-Stowaway | High (date). The reasons are lore only. |
| 3.3 | Arrived in **Denver in April 1872** and worked as a **gardener** for about a month. MC-Stowaway says he rode the new railroad west and worked on it to pay his way. | WP, HC-Kids, CE | High |
| 3.4 | On **May 1, 1872**, he bought a partnership in **John Staderman's bottling business** in Denver, and he was sole owner by the end of 1872. HC-Kids describes him as "manager of a bottle plant, six days a week". | WP, CE, FU, HC-Kids | High |

## 4. Golden, 1873: the brewery

| # | Claim | Sources | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | On **November 14, 1873**, Coors and **Jacob Schueler** bought the abandoned **Golden City Tannery** in Golden, Colorado Territory, on the banks of **Clear Creek** at the foot of Table Mountain. | WP, HC-Coors caption ("an old tannery along the banks of Clear Creek"), CE, CBHF, Encyclopedia.com | High |
| 4.2 | Schueler was a **Denver confectioner**: candy-store or ice-cream-parlour owner, a fellow German, and one of the bottling business's best customers. ⚠ CE spells the name "Scheuler". | WP, CE, MC-Stowaway | High |
| 4.3 | Money: Schueler put in **$18,000** and Coors his **$2,000** savings, out of the roughly **$20,000** needed to buy and convert the tannery. | FaG ($18,000 / $2,000); FU ("Schueler invested the lion's share of the $20,000") | Medium-High. FU confirms the $20,000 total and that Schueler paid most of it. The exact 18/2 split comes from FaG and the family histories, which are widely repeated. |
| 4.4 | **Name:** "**Golden Brewery**" (WP, HC-Kids, CBHF), also given as "Schueler & Coors" (MC-Stowaway: "Schueler & Coors Brewing Co."; CE: "Scheuler and Coors Brewing Company"). An 1880s print in the Golden History Museum is titled "The Golden Brewery". | WP, MC-Stowaway, CE | High (Golden Brewery) |
| 4.5 | Beer was in production by **February 1874** and on sale from early **April 1874**. CE gives the output as **800 gallons a day** by 1874. | WP, CE | High (1874). Medium (800 gal). |
| 4.6 | Water: Coors insisted on Colorado spring water. The "Brewed with pure Rocky Mountain spring water" slogan dates from the **1930s** (ads from 1937). ⚠ Do not imply the 1873 founders used that slogan. | FU, HC-Coors | High |

## 5. 1879–1913 milestones

| # | Claim | Sources | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | **Buyout of Schueler: 1880** (WP, MC-Stowaway, HC-Kids, FU). The brewery was renamed **Adolph Coors Golden Brewery**. ⚠ CE dates the buyout to **1879**. | WP, MC-Stowaway, FU vs CE | High (1880) |
| 5.2 | Married **Louisa Weber** on **April 12, 1879**, at his home on the brewery grounds. They raised six children. A Molson Coors archive portrait of young Adolph is filed as "young-adolph-April-12-1879", which suggests it is his wedding portrait. | WP, CE, MC image filename | High |
| 5.3 | Output grew from **7,049 barrels (1887)** to **17,600 barrels (1890)**. | FU | Medium |
| 5.4 | **Medal winner at the Chicago World's Fair** (World's Columbian Exposition, **1893**). CE also says he was a millionaire by 1890. | CE | Medium (single source) |
| 5.5 | **1890 strike:** workers won a 30-cent raise and a guaranteed ten-hour day. | HC-Coors | Medium |
| 5.6 | Rail: the Colorado Central ran through Golden, and the brewery had its own spur. By April 1933 there was a daily freight service to the brewery. | DPL X-10034 caption, GT | Medium |
| 5.7 | Incorporated in **1913** as the Adolph Coors Brewing and Manufacturing Company. Renamed the **Adolph Coors Company** in 1920. | FU | Medium |
| 5.8 | Before Prohibition, the brewery bottled about **74,000 barrels a year** of what became Banquet beer. | 5280 | Medium |

## 6. Prohibition (Colorado went dry first)

| # | Claim | Sources | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Colorado voters approved statewide prohibition on **November 3, 1914**: **123,589 for (51.2%)** to **118,017 against (48.8%)**. ⚠ HC-Final and the Colorado Virtual Library print "129,589", which looks like a typo. The official SOS table says 123,589. Golden voted against. | SOS, HC-Final | High |
| 6.2 | Prohibition took effect at **midnight, January 1, 1916**, four years before national Prohibition (1920). It lasted **17 years** in Colorado. | HC-Final, 5280, D7, MC-Prohibition | High |
| 6.3 | Of Colorado's **44** pre-Prohibition breweries, only **4** survived. Coors and Tivoli-Union were the only two ready to ship beer on April 7, 1933. | HC-Coors, HC-Final | High |
| 6.4 | Legend says the beer on hand was **poured into Clear Creek** in late 1915. ⚠ This is unverified lore. The Coors archivist says only "they had to simply get rid of their product." | D7 | Low (as fact) |
| 6.5 | **Malted milk:** Grover Coors adapted the brewing equipment to turn about **500 gallons of local milk a day** into malted milk. It was sold to **Mars** (5280) and **Hershey** (D7), and advertised as "next to mother's milk the best food for infants". A 1918 newspaper ad survives. Production continued until **1957** (5280) or "into the 1950s" (D7). | 5280, D7, MC-Prohibition, HC-Coors, Wikimedia Commons ad (1918) | High (malted milk). Medium (buyers, end date). |
| 6.6 | **Near beer "Mannah":** a non-alcoholic cereal beverage under 0.5% ABV, made to keep a skeleton crew employed. | 5280, HC-Kids, D7 (near beer, unnamed) | Medium-High |
| 6.7 | **Porcelain:** Adolph Coors took ownership of the **Herold China and Pottery Co.** in Golden in **1913** (MC-Prohibition); D7 says 1912. It was renamed **Coors Porcelain Co.** in 1920 and used Golden clay deposits. Products included tea sets, cookware, spark plugs and laboratory porcelain (mortars, crucibles). It became **CoorsTek**. | MC-Prohibition, WP, D7, Golden History Museum objects | High. ⚠ Founding year given as 1912 or 1913. |
| 6.8 | **Cement:** HC-Kids and CBHF list cement among Prohibition products. | HC-Kids, CBHF | Low-Medium. Only these two (related) sources. Not in WP, the MC blog or 5280. Use with care. |
| 6.9 | The company was not profitable during Prohibition. Archivist Heidi Harris: "They were not making a profit." | D7 | Medium |

## 7. Repeal and Adolph's death

| # | Claim | Sources | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | 🔒 Adolph Coors died on **June 5, 1929**, aged 82, **four years before repeal**. He fell from a sixth-floor window of the **Cavalier Hotel, Virginia Beach**. Newspapers of the day gave conflicting accounts, and no autopsy was done. It is often described as a possible suicide, while CE states flatly that he jumped. He was reportedly recovering from influenza; the room is given as 602 or 606. **Sensitive.** Suggest the film says only "Adolph died in 1929, four years before beer came back." | WP, CE, CVM, Visit Virginia Beach, HC-Kids | High (date and place). Cause uncertain. |
| 7.2 | The **Cullen–Harrison Act** (3.2% beer), signed by FDR on March 22, 1933, took effect on **April 7, 1933**. Colorado passed matching law, and 3.2 beer was legal in Colorado that day. | HC-Final, LOC photo "F.D. Roosevelt signing beer bill, March 22?, 1933" | High |
| 7.3 | At **12:01 a.m., Friday April 7, 1933**, a special Colorado & Southern train of **21 refrigerator cars** carrying **18,000+ cases** left the brewery. At the same time, **more than 70 loaded delivery trucks** drove out, escorted by a Jefferson County Sheriff patrol. ⚠ The *Colorado Transcript* had announced "more than 100 trucks" the day before. | HC-Coors, GT | High (train). Medium (truck count: say "dozens of trucks" or "70+"). |
| 7.4 | That week **750,000 empty bottles** arrived, and **125 men** worked around the clock. By April 20, 1933, the brewery was making **500 barrels a day**. | HC-Coors, GT | High |
| 7.5 | Full national repeal came with the **21st Amendment, ratified December 5, 1933**. Utah was the deciding state, and Colorado had ratified in September 1933. | HC-Final | High |
| 7.6 | ⚠ "Prohibition ended in 1933" is correct nationally, but for Coors the dry years ran from **1916 to 1933 (17 years)**, not the national 13. | 5280, D7, MC-Prohibition | High |

## 9. Commonly misstated or disputed points

1. **Port of arrival** (Baltimore vs New York). The company itself says Baltimore.
2. **"Fireman"** means a steam-engine stoker, not a firefighter.
3. **Buyout year:** 1880 per most sources, 1879 per the Colorado Encyclopedia.
4. **Spelling:** Schueler, also printed Scheuler. Original surname Kuhrs or Kohrs.
5. **Colorado prohibition vote:** 1914 (not 1915 or 1916). The official yes count is 123,589, not 129,589.
6. **Coors Porcelain founding:** 1912, 1913 or 1910 (when Herold founded the pottery). The rename to Coors Porcelain was in 1920.
7. **Dumping beer into Clear Creek** is legend, not documented.
8. **Cement** as a Prohibition product is weakly sourced.
9. **Death:** suicide is not established. Treat it as sensitive.
10. **1933 truck count:** 70+ according to History Colorado's account of the event, and 100+ in the newspaper's advance notice.
